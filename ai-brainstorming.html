AI Brainstorming

A few of the questions I asked AI, to show how I used it.

Is bulk sorting done only in some conditions? What do you think is the customer's pain? Claims keep coming in: is it bulk, too many at once, or does every claim go through it? Or is it complexity in sorting, and the 3.4% might not be resolved with executable logic?

Bulk sorting: the brief only says it "lets a handler sort many claims at once", so it's a selection tool: pick many claims, put them all in one pile. A handler would only do that when the claims are obviously alike, for example 300 windscreen claims after a hailstorm, or every claim under £500 from one policy type. So yes, it's useful only under certain conditions: high volume of similar, low-judgement claims. It does nothing for a claim that needs reading.

What I think the pain is: "automatic sorting" is one phrase covering two different problems, and the data points at both.

Problem A: every claim goes through a human, even the obvious ones. 2.1M claims, most sorted in minutes. If, say, 60-70% are plainly Simple, that's over a million decisions a year where a handler adds nothing but time. Insurers feel this as handler cost, and it's almost certainly what the competitor is selling and what Sales hears in deals. The 11-minute median includes queue time, so the real wasted effort is probably smaller than it looks, but it's real. Automation fits this problem well, and the risk is low because getting a Simple claim wrong costs little.

Problem B: some claims are hard. The 15% over four hours and the 3.4% that move piles. The 3.4% is probably not fixable with executable logic: the claim changes after the first sort. A document arrives, a question gets answered, an engineer's report comes back. The first sort was right on the information available. That's not a sorting failure, it's a timing problem. The fix there is workflow: flag what's missing, re-check when new information lands, route to the right handler. A model can't know what hasn't arrived yet.

The 15% is the least certain. It could be complexity, missing information, or just no handler being free. Those are three different problems and you'd need the data to split them.

View, with confidence:

  • Medium confidence the primary pain is Problem A: handler time spent on obvious claims, made urgent by a competitor now offering to remove it.
  • Medium confidence the 3.4% is mostly late information, not bad sorting, and shouldn't be the target of automation.
  • Low confidence about what drives the 15%; that's the first data question.

If this holds, the product shape follows: automate the obvious Simple pile first with a human able to override, and treat the hard claims as a workflow problem, not a prediction problem. Bulk sorting sits awkwardly in between: it helps with Problem A only when claims arrive in visible clusters, which is why finding out who asked for it matters.

The thing to check before trusting any of this: whether the customers asking for automation are high-volume insurers (Problem A) or ones with long settlement times (Problem B). The brief doesn't say, and the answer changes everything.

Build checklist:

  • Per-customer, versioned sorting logic with plain-language templates. Not code, not one global model.
  • Rules for policy, AI for judgement. AI suggests only inside the rules.
  • Ladder: shadow, suggest, auto-sort Simple, then Needs a closer look. Suspicious is always a human decision.
  • Handler control: one click accept, one click change with a reason. Visible "sorted by system" list, daily sample check, kill switch per customer and pile.
  • Append-only audit log: pile, who or what decided, reason, logic version, time. Plain-language explanation on demand.
  • Any logic change or retrain is replayed on real claims and signed off before live.
  • Vulnerability moves a claim up, never down. Bias tests on anything feeding Suspicious.
  • Re-sort prompt on new information. Missing-info flags and document summaries for complex claims.
  • Tell handler managers that claims per handler will fall and the queue will get harder.

Not doing: automatic Suspicious. One global model. Auto-sort without a shadow-mode result. Automation with no plain-language reason. Building what the deals literally ask for before asking what they mean.

What is the number the COO watches?

It's my phrase from the first row of the table: "Handler cost per claim is the number the COO watches." I meant the insurer's cost to handle each claim, worked out roughly as total claims-operations cost (mostly handler salaries) divided by the number of claims handled.

Doesn't that change the solution we build? If the sorting logic changes, how does our code handle that?

Yes, and it's the thing most likely to sink a naive build.

The problem. If each of 180 insurers has its own sorting logic, and that logic changes over time (new fraud patterns, a regulator's ruling, a new product line), then a single model trained on everyone's historical claims is wrong for most customers on day one and gets more wrong every month. That's probably part of why the 18-month-old prototype matters less than it looks: a global predictor doesn't fit a per-customer problem.

How the code handles it: logic is data, not code.

  • The sorting logic lives outside the application code, as versioned configuration per customer. Changing a rule is a config change with an audit trail, not a deploy. The customer's claims-ops lead, or Customer Success on their behalf, can change it.
  • Two layers. A rules layer that is deterministic and owned by the customer ("under £500, photo attached, no claim in 12 months: Simple"). A suggestion layer, the model, that works inside the boundaries the rules set. Rules are the guardrail; the model never overrides a rule. Every decision traces to a rule version, a model version, or a handler.
  • Every decision records which version of the logic made it. The regulator asks about a claim from eight months ago; you show the rules as they were then, not as they are now.
  • Replay before switching on. When logic changes, run the last few months of that customer's claims through the new version and show what would have sorted differently. That's the eval harness, and the same harness decides whether automation is safe.
  • Drift monitoring per customer per version. Override rate and re-sort rate rising for one customer means their logic has gone stale, or their claims have changed. That's the signal to review it.
  • Model retraining is a logic change too. Same versioning, same replay gate, same sign-off.

What that does to the product. It stops being "a model that predicts the pile" and becomes a sorting-logic platform: rules, suggestions, explanations, versioning and evaluation, with a model as one component. More engineering up front and less data science, which matters for a squad of five engineers and no data scientist.

Yes, that is the right check, with one refinement. Time to open till time to sort gives you active handling time. You also need the step before it: arrival to first open, which is queue wait. Four timestamps per claim (arrived, first opened, sorted, re-sorted) and you can split the whole 15%.

And yes, Keelwater has the pile counts, because the piles live in Keelwater. If most of the 2.1M are Simple, that is the strongest single piece of evidence for the first slice, and it costs one query.

Two checks I added that you had not mentioned:

  • Do handlers at the same insurer agree? Similar claims, different handlers, different piles. It is the only way to test "the logic lives in their heads" with data rather than a story.
  • Rerun everything for the customers who are asking. The 180-customer averages could be hiding that the six deals look nothing like the median.

Before anything is automated, what would a prototype look like for getting customer feedback? Is a prototype even the right answer here?

Yes, but it tests the workflow, not whether the automation is safe. A realistic screen can show whether handlers understand a suggestion, trust it, and know when not to. It cannot prove the rule or model behind it is right. That comes from testing on past claims.

Show a handler a few realistic claims: the suggested pile, a plain-language reason, the evidence, anything missing, and accept or change with a reason. Slip in a few suggestions that are deliberately wrong, and watch whether handlers catch them or click through.

There is no AI at this step, so how do we check for fraud before a Simple rule runs?

With data checks, not a model. Is there a risk flag on the claim? Is the same phone number or address on other open claims? Those run on every claim before any Simple rule.

Claims bunching just under a threshold, such as £495 against a £500 rule, are different. They are not visible on one claim. They are a pattern you watch regularly across all claims.

Do we need a different model for every customer? Is that feasible? And what does a different model even mean: is it different prompting?

It does not need to be 180 separately trained models. That would be hard to build, test and maintain. The same underlying model can serve every customer. What changes per customer is the rules they approve, their thresholds, and the instructions and reference material the model is given.

What has to be per customer is the evaluation. Each customer's results are tested on their own claims before anything is shown to their handlers.
